My [previous article](/writing/the-weight-of-a-conjunction/) began with a conjunction. In Rousseau's most famous sentence,<br />
<span lang="fr">*L'homme est né libre, et partout il est dans les fers.*</span>[^1]<br />
the et resolves nothing. It does not say that man was born free but is now in chains, nor that he is in chains because he was born free. It sets the two states beside each other and leaves them there.

A sentence built that way is not a complaint. It states a condition and hands over a question: within what kind of bonds can a being born free live without falling under another will? That is where this article begins. *Du contrat social* is not a book of escape. Rousseau does not ask how to get out of society, law, or political obligation. He asks whether, taking men as they are and laws as they might be, there can be a legitimate and reliable rule of political administration.

## The Problem Before the Answer: Dependence and Inequality

*Du contrat social* does not begin in a vacuum, however abrupt its first sentence sounds. Behind it stands the *Discours sur l'origine et les fondements de l'inégalité parmi les hommes*, where Rousseau treats human social existence not as a natural necessity but as a historical and moral transformation. The question there is not only how human beings came together, but how their coming together turned into inequality, dependence, and finally domination.[^2]

Socialisation reshapes desire and places human beings in relations of comparison. The person who lives among others is already formed by property, hierarchy, recognition, and the need to exist in the eyes of others. Dependence of that kind costs freedom without any use of force, working through the structure of the relations themselves. The chain acquires a moral meaning wherever one person lives under the arbitrary will of another.

The Second Discourse asks how dependence arose. *Du contrat social* asks whether, inside a condition already spoiled by it, a legitimate political order can be constituted. Origin gives way to legitimacy.

## Why the Answer Cannot Be a Return to Nature

The obvious misreading is that Rousseau wants us back in the forest. The state of nature does real work in his thought, but the work is critical rather than programmatic: it is the measure against which existing society can be judged, and it makes the chains of that society visible. It is not a destination. Rousseau says plainly that he takes men as they are and laws as they can be.[^3]

The social pact exists to turn a bond that enslaves into one that does not, and establishing that difference is its whole task.

## The Social Pact: Giving Oneself to the Whole

"Contract" is a misleading word here if it suggests an ordinary legal agreement. Rousseau is not looking for a compromise among people who each want to protect a separate interest. He asks how human beings can form a political whole without handing themselves over to anyone.

The formula runs:

> a form of association that will defend and protect the person and goods of each member with the whole common force, while allowing each individual, in uniting with all, to obey only himself and remain as free as before.[^4]

The demand is exorbitant, and Rousseau presents it as a problem to be solved rather than as a description of anything that exists. Union and freedom are required at once: the whole common force on one side, obedience to no one but oneself on the other.

His solution turns on where the individual gives himself. To a person, a king, a class, a private group: that is slavery. To the whole, which is nothing but all the individuals together, and on terms identical for everyone, so that no one becomes anyone's private master.

Saying "I will obey you" and joining a whole in which one is also a member differ not in the quantity of obedience but in what is being obeyed, and everything depends on it: if the difference holds, law can be more than a command; if it does not, the rest of *Du contrat social* is an elaborate way of arranging chains.

## The General Will: Law Without Private Domination

The pact binds the individual to the whole. But "the whole" does not yet act. A society can be founded, legislate, and maintain an orderly government while driving its members into deeper dependence. By which will, then, does the political body act?

Rousseau's answer is the general will, directed to the common good of the body itself — not the sum of private desires, the interest of a class, or the preference of a temporary majority.

A law may express the interest of the powerful, the security of the wealthy, the anger of a majority, or the personal will of those who govern, and remain a law in form. What it produces is a tidier chain, the individual no longer under a master's command but under rules that carry the master's will.

Rousseau's own distinction between the general will and the will of all is where this becomes difficult.[^5] The will of all can be a sum of private interests. Many people may want the same thing, and the thing they want may still be the interest of a part rather than of the whole. A vote declares the general will only when citizens judge by the common good rather than sorting themselves into factions that vote as blocs. Legitimacy does not come from numbers: no procedure can certify its own result. Counting tells you what was willed, not whether what was willed was general.

The *Discourse on Political Economy* presses the same thought from another direction. Rousseau separates the government of a state from the government of a family. Paternal authority rests on something real, since the father's strength, for as long as his children need it, is for Rousseau a genuine natural foundation of authority. Among adults in political society there is no such foundation, because human beings are naturally equal; authority there can rest only on conventions and laws.[^6] Citizens are not the ruler's children, and fatherhood, force, tradition and superiority cannot supply what a common legal order supplies.

## From Natural Freedom to Civil Freedom

Entry into political society cannot leave freedom as it was. Natural freedom is the range of what an individual can do by his own power and desire. Unlimited in principle, it reaches exactly as far as his strength and stops where another's begins. It is not the sort of thing that can be carried intact into a common life.

The pact exchanges rather than preserves freedom. What is lost is natural freedom and an unlimited right to whatever one can obtain. What is gained is civil freedom and property in what one possesses.[^7]

Civil freedom is the condition of living inside a legal order one belongs to, without being subject to anyone's arbitrary will, rather than the ability to do as one likes. In the state of nature the limit of freedom is force. In the civil state the limit is common law.

Obedience to a law one has prescribed to oneself is freedom.[^8]

Rousseau does not mean that whatever one wants is law. The citizen participates in making common law as a member of the sovereign, so that obedience is membership in an order one helps constitute rather than submission to another person.

Property shows the exchange concretely. Possession in the natural condition is holding something for as long as one can hold it. In political society it becomes a recognised and protected right. The individual surrenders unlimited claims and receives secured ones, provided the law falls equally on everyone. Where some live as legislators and others merely as subjects of the law, the exchange has not taken place, and what is obeyed is again a private will, in legal dress.

## The Fragility of Legitimate Bonds

That could be where the argument ends: the pact binds the individual to the whole, the general will supplies the common good, law converts natural freedom into civil freedom. Rousseau does not let it end there, and the reason is not modesty about his own construction. Nothing in the pact keeps working by itself.

The danger is already inside the distinction that solved the problem. Individuals and groups do not reliably will the common good; they will their own good and describe it as everyone's. A faction, an economic interest, a governing class can present a private will as the will of the body, and the law can go on operating under that name without any claim on obedience.

Rousseau says as much in the *Political Economy*: in any society, the public interest and the laws have no natural force of their own and are under continual attack from the personal interest and passions of rulers and members alike.[^9] No natural force of their own. The phrase concedes that the whole construction is held up by nothing except the continued disposition of citizens to hold it up.

Private wills can capture the public sphere over time; government, which exists to execute the sovereign's will, can begin to substitute its own; law can go on being called law while it protects particular interests, without any of the founding documents changing. To say "there is a general will, therefore the law is legitimate" inverts the claim. The law is legitimate for as long as it expresses the general will, and no longer.

The image from the first article returns here with more force than it had at the start. Rousseau's aim was never to make chains more orderly, and a legitimate bond and a well-arranged chain are hard to tell apart from inside, since the change does not announce itself.

This fragility is not a flaw discovered late in the argument. It shows how demanding the actual conditions are: equality, a common good citizens can recognise as such, and a government kept subordinate to the people who authorise it. Rousseau supplies no mechanism that keeps these conditions in place. The reconciliation of obedience and freedom holds only for as long as they do.

## Conclusion: Freedom Through a Legitimate Bond

Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains.

The chain in that sentence was never the name for every bond. It names the one kind that destroys freedom: dependence on a will that is not answerable to those it binds.

What Rousseau's answer changes is not whether one is bound but what one is bound to: not a master, but a law one had a hand in making, and freedom through that kind of bond is only as durable as the bond stays legitimate. That is not something the founding settles. It has to be asked again.
