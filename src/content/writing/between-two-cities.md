There is a painting at Istanbul Modern that I keep going back to. Large — two metres by two and a half — grey and ochre, with shapes that never quite become anything recognizable. The title is *Studio*. The artist is Albert Bitran, and it took me longer than I'd like to admit to find out who he was.

[View the painting on Istanbul Modern's collection page](https://www.istanbulmodern.org/en/collection/studio)

Bitran was born in Istanbul in 1931, into a Sephardic Jewish family in Pangaltı. He was multilingual — Turkish, French, Ladino, and English — educated at the French Collège Saint-Michel, with summers spent on the Princes' Islands. In 1948, he left for Paris to study architecture at the École Spéciale, and lasted less than a year — the city's postwar art scene pulled him away from the drafting table. He moved into the Cité Universitaire, where Jesús Rafael Soto and Serge Poliakoff were also living, and started painting instead.

His first solo show was in 1951. The writer and critic Henri-Pierre Roché became an early supporter, giving him lodging and materials. Another critic, Charles Estienne, gave him something more useful than patronage. As Jean-Luc Chalumeau later recalled, Estienne told him, roughly:

> "You don't have the head to be a geometric abstractionist."

He was right, apparently, because Bitran left the precise geometric work of his early years behind and moved toward something looser and more atmospheric — the direction that would define the rest of his career.

He took French citizenship in 1958. He died in Paris in 2018. His work is in the Centre Pompidou, the Musée d'Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, the Henie Onstad Kunstsenter, and — through a private donation — Istanbul Modern.

---

The painting belongs to the postwar current usually called Lyrical Abstraction — painting that treats gesture and material process as the substance of the work rather than as a means to an image. Bitran worked within this current for decades, often painting on several canvases at once, layering pigment, sometimes gluing paper onto the canvas (a technique called marouflage) as part of building up a surface.

*Studio* — *Atölye*, *L'Atelier* — was painted in 1959. Bitran had recently married, had recently become a French citizen, and had recently moved into a studio on rue des Plantes in the 14th arrondissement. Istanbul Modern lists it as mixed media on canvas, 200 × 250 cm, donated by Ceyda and Ünal Göğüş.

The painting doesn't have an easel or a window or a figure in it. Light comes from somewhere above the frame, and toward the bottom there might be a floor, though it's hard to be sure where one plane ends and another begins. A couple of vertical shapes stand in the middle of the canvas — they could be walls, or they could just be marks that happen to run upright rather than across. Bitran described the rhythm of these atelier works as "a journey of shadows."

He said the greys, the dusty dark tones, and the yellow ochres in his work came from the Istanbul of his childhood, and described black as part of his Ottoman cultural inheritance. I look at *Studio* and think about a life split between two cities anyway — that might say more about what I'm looking for than about what Bitran put there.

He left the rue des Plantes studio in 1968 for one on rue Notre-Dame-des-Champs, and worked in others later still. He never stopped, as far as I can tell, being from Istanbul.
