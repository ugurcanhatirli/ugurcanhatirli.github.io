## Reading Rousseau Across French, English, and Turkish

The reading that follows compares Rousseau's most famous statement across three editions: the French original, Victor Gourevitch's English translation, and Vedat Günyol's Turkish translation.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *Du contrat social*, GF Flammarion. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract and Other Later Political Writings*, edited and translated by Victor Gourevitch, Cambridge University Press. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *Toplum Sözleşmesi*, translated by Vedat Günyol, Türkiye İş Bankası Kültür Yayınları.

Reading intellectual historian Richard Whatmore's *The History of Political Thought: A Very Short Introduction*, I came across Rousseau's most famous statement in the form: "Man is born free but is everywhere in chains." It was this seemingly minor choice of conjunction that sent me back to the French original: <span lang="fr">"L'homme est né libre, et partout il est dans les fers."</span>

Rousseau writes "et," not "mais." The choice of "but" in some English translations is understandable — it makes the contrast already implicit in the sentence more explicit — but "et" means "and," and the contrast Rousseau builds is one of juxtaposition, not opposition. Had he wanted the conjunction itself to carry the tension, he had "mais" available and did not use it. The more literal, and to my mind more faithful, rendering is the one Gourevitch uses here: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains."

Vedat Günyol's Turkish — <span lang="tr">"İnsan özgür doğar, oysa her yerde zincire vurulmuştur."</span> — is fluent and aphoristic, but "oysa" resolves the tension the same way "but" does. And where Günyol has <span lang="tr">"İnsan özgür doğar,"</span> Rousseau's "est né libre" could also be read more literally as <span lang="tr">"özgür doğmuştur"</span>: the first sounds general and aphoristic, the second marks a condition dating from birth rather than a birthright asserted in the present tense.

"Partout" does its own work: not "here," not "in some places," but everywhere, which keeps the statement from settling into a complaint about one regime or one moment.

"Dans les fers" is usually "in chains," but "fers" literally means irons, and carries the older, harsher image of shackles — a nuance a translation might reasonably leave to a footnote rather than force into the main text.

## From Historical Ignorance to Political Legitimacy

On its own, the sentence reads as a definite, almost aphoristic judgment. What follows it does not.

<span lang="fr">"Comment ce changement s'est-il fait? Je l'ignore. Qu'est-ce qui peut le rendre légitime? Je crois pouvoir résoudre cette question."</span> Rousseau asks how the change came about and answers himself with <span lang="fr">"Je l'ignore"</span> — accurately enough rendered as "I do not know" or <span lang="tr">"Bilmiyorum,"</span> though neither quite lands the same way. "Ignorer" sounds colder to me than an ordinary admission of not knowing; it reads less like modesty than refusal. <span lang="tr">"Bilmiyorum"</span> holds some of that same chill; the English softens it.

Rousseau states a universal condition and declines to explain its history. His interest lies elsewhere — not in how the change occurred but in whether it can be made legitimate. The opening sentence is not, then, simply a record of a fall from freedom into bondage: man is born free, and finds himself already inside political, social, and legal bonds that were there before him. The question the text goes on to ask is what kind of bonds these can legitimately be.

That the sentence carries restriction, dependence, domination is plain enough. What is less obvious is that *Du contrat social* moves toward examining these bonds rather than escaping them.

## Dans les fers: Constraint and Domination

Rousseau continues: <span lang="fr">"Tel se croit le maître des autres, qui ne laisse pas d'être plus esclave qu'eux."</span> "Tel" is not a particular person — a king, a specific master — but a type: whoever believes himself master of others. And <span lang="fr">"ne laisse pas d'être,"</span> in older literary French, means something like "does not fail to be" or "remains nonetheless" — so that the sentence unfolds as: such a person believes himself master of others, and nonetheless is more enslaved than they are.

The person who believes himself master may not be free either. He may, despite his sense of his own superiority, be "more enslaved" precisely because of the relation in which he stands to those he rules. To have power over others is not, for Rousseau, proof of freedom.

## Can Political Bonds Become Legitimate?

Human beings cannot simply live outside social and political life; the question is what kind of bonds hold them there. Dependence on another person's arbitrary will does not make one free. An order established collectively, valid for all, reducible to no one's private will, is a different matter — for Rousseau, only the general will can direct the forces of the state toward the common good.[^1] Obedience within such an order is not submission to a person or a faction but participation in something held in common.

Rousseau's social contract sets itself the task of finding "a form of association" through which each, "uniting with all," nevertheless "remain[s] as free as before."[^2] Becoming part of a whole without falling under anyone's private will is the paradox at the centre of his account of legitimate political order: freedom, on this account, is not the absence of law but the law one recognises as one's own, given through the common will one is part of.

Freedom is carried, on these terms, from a natural form into a political one. Natural freedom rests on an individual's own power and desire to act, and cannot survive political life unaltered. What the social contract offers instead is civil freedom: not being subject to another's arbitrary will, and belonging to a law that binds everyone equally.

## Conclusion: The Weight of a Conjunction

What began as a small difference in translation — "et" rendered as "but" — turns out to carry more than that difference usually would. Rousseau's "and" does not resolve the tension between freedom and chains; it leaves the two side by side, for the reader to work through. "But," or "oysa," does that work for you, and in doing so gives something away.

Man is born free and nevertheless finds himself everywhere in irons — not free and then stripped of it, but free and in chains as one condition, stated at once. <span lang="fr">"Je l'ignore"</span> refuses to explain how that came about, and the refusal is itself a redirection: not toward the history of the change, but toward the question of what bonds a being born free can still live under without losing that freedom entirely.

[^1]: French original: <span lang="fr">"La volonté générale peut seule diriger les forces de l'État selon la fin de son institution, qui est le bien commun."</span> *Du contrat social*, Book II, Chapter I.
[^2]: French original: <span lang="fr">"Trouver une forme d'association qui défende et protège de toute la force commune la personne et les biens de chaque associé, et par laquelle chacun s'unissant à tous n'obéisse pourtant qu'à lui-même et reste aussi libre qu'auparavant."</span> *Du contrat social*, Book I, Chapter VI. Gourevitch translates this as: "To find a form of association that will defend and protect the person and the goods of each associate with the full common force, and by means of which each, uniting with all, nevertheless obey only himself and remain as free as before."
