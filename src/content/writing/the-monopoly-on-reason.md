*Reason as Doctrine in Ayn Rand*

---

### Introduction — The Appeal and the Danger

Ayn Rand's television interview with James McConnell on 15 May 1961, given on the occasion of the publication of *For the New Intellectual*, presents her thought with unusual clarity.[1] She does not merely express a handful of political views. Fiction, philosophy, human types, intellectual history, capitalism, America, and happiness are brought together within a single moral narrative. Both the appeal and the danger of the interview lie in this unity.

Much of what she says deserves to be taken seriously. Emotions alone cannot make a claim true. Physical force cannot replace persuasion. A person may legitimately regard their own life as a value. Entrepreneurial organization plays a real part in turning a scientific discovery into something available to society. For someone governed by guilt, ashamed of personal desires, or accustomed to living according to the judgment of others, the thought that "your life belongs to you" can be liberating.

Rand keeps doing the same thing with a distinction that starts out reasonable: she treats it as absolute, and lets it explain a whole field. Emotions are not evidence, and so a person who acts on emotion is treated as having no way to relate to reality or to other people at all. Something like this happens again in her account of science, capitalism, America, and happiness.

Behind these transitions lies a double use of a single word. In one sense reason is a method: attending to facts, clarifying concepts, demanding justification, testing inferences. Understood this way it can provide common ground for inquiry. In the other sense, "reason" is the name for the conclusions of Objectivism—a particular metaphysics, an egoist ethics, an ideal of the productive individual, individual rights, laissez-faire capitalism. Someone who does not reach Rand's conclusions risks being read as having departed from reason itself.

---

### I. The Intellectual Position Rand Defines for Herself

McConnell asks Rand whether she sees herself primarily as a novelist or as a philosopher. Her answer is unequivocal:

"I would say I am primarily both, equally and for the same reason."[1, p. 2]

Academic accounts likewise treat her as a novelist-philosopher who constructed an extensive system running from epistemology to aesthetics.[2] Her fundamental purpose in both literature and philosophy, she says, is to define and present the "ideal man" in concrete form. The novel stages the ideal human being; philosophy establishes the premises that make such a person possible. Literature becomes the dramatic demonstration of a predetermined human model, philosophy the theoretical apparatus supporting it.

She claims both identities with equal force and then explains why both serve the same ideal. The claim comes first; the justification follows.

Elsewhere she says that when she began writing and studying philosophy she found herself in profound disagreement with "all the existing philosophies," particularly with their moral codes.[1, p. 2] Much of philosophy advances by questioning inherited values, but radically different and mutually conflicting traditions are gathered here into a single inadequate whole, with no qualification to limit the word "all."

She then repeatedly uses the expression "had to." She had to develop her own thought, define her own system, identify the convictions that would produce the character of an ideal human being. Someone dissatisfied with existing philosophies may reasonably develop an independent view. What the language of necessity adds is the sense that every existing philosophy had failed, and that no possibility remained except that Rand should fill the void.

For as long as she can remember, she says, she has held the same fundamental philosophy; over the years she has expanded her knowledge of details, definitions, and applications, but has never needed to alter any of her fundamentals.[1, p. 2] Convictions can be held for decades because they have been tested against new information and criticism and have survived. What she describes instead is the progressive elaboration of a system already correct from the beginning.

Philosophy here becomes less an activity in which a person tests the possibility of being wrong than the consistent expansion of a truth grasped early. A system may be internally ordered and still rest on mistaken premises. Her later judgments—on Plato, Kant, existentialism, modern left-wing intellectuals—are the judgments of someone classifying other systems by standards she does not submit to examination. Learning takes place, but the possibility that she may have been fundamentally mistaken is almost absent.

### II. Producer, Witch Doctor, Attila

Rand's philosophy consists not only of propositions but of character types: the philosopher, the professional intellectual, the New Intellectual, the Producer, the Witch Doctor, and Attila. These categories function less as analytical tools than as roles already filled with moral judgment.

The philosopher determines the fundamental ideas of a culture; professional intellectuals are "field agents" who carry those ideas into their areas of expertise and into society. The philosopher is the commander-in-chief of the army, intellectuals its transmission belts, and it is the philosopher who determines history.[1, pp. 2–3] Ideas do have social consequences; law, politics, education, and art are shaped by assumptions about reality and human nature. But economic structures, technologies, institutions, wars, class relations, and everyday practices also affect which ideas arise and how they change, and intellectuals interpret, criticize, adapt, and sometimes transform what they receive. Rand's scheme glorifies intellectual activity while depriving the intellectual of independent thought.

The New Intellectual does not escape the hierarchy. The old intellectuals have betrayed reason, denigrated the businessman, and supplied theoretical legitimacy to tyranny; the new one is to reunite reason, production, and individual rights. Independent thought is demanded, and it is also expected to arrive at the Randian principles presented as the "minimum" requirements of civilization. What is new is the role assigned to replace the old intellectual, not the outcome of thought.

The Witch Doctor treats emotions and beliefs as guides to reality and, fearing the physical world, seeks a mystical realm in place of facts. Attila despises abstraction and lives by plunder and force. Historical cultures, Rand argues, have been governed by an alliance between them: the Witch Doctor supplies intellectual legitimacy, Attila the coercive power that protects him. People do substitute desire for evidence, and political power does seek sacred or theoretical legitimation. Rand goes further and claims to know the psychology of her opponent—he fears reality, cannot cope with facts, seeks escape, depends on a tyrant for material survival. These motives are not demonstrated in the interview. They are built into the definition of the type.

"Attila" becomes an instrument of political condemnation. Rand describes the left-wing intellectuals of her time as "neo-mystics" and "philosophical Attila-ists" who supply philosophical justification for the rule of dictators such as Khrushchev.[1, p. 5] Attila is first identified with plunder, enslavement, and thoughtlessness; opposing views are then associated with Attila, and what those views actually defend no longer needs examination.

Opposite these stands the Producer, who is not only someone who manufactures material goods but anyone who creates scientific, intellectual, or philosophical value—in this broad sense Aristotle is a Producer too. As the narrative develops, the historical centre of the ideal shifts toward the businessman who converts discovery into a product, organizes mass production, and raises the standard of living.

Opponents are named by pejoratives—Witch Doctor, Attila, mystic, bankrupt intellectual—while those Rand endorses are named by their function. To call someone a Producer already implies that the person is creative, rational, and admirable; to call someone an Attila-ist establishes proximity to tyranny before any argument begins. A real person can be productive and dependent at once, or rational and afraid of the same thing; institutions are no easier to reduce to a single type.

### III. The War Between Aristotle and Plato

In Rand's historical narrative Aristotle is not merely a great philosopher. He is the symbol of reason, logic, science, production, and progress. Plato, Plotinus, and Augustine belong to a lineage of mysticism. When Aquinas revives Aristotle, reason returns and the path to the Renaissance and the modern world opens. The narrative compresses a long history into two genealogies, compelling for the same reason it misleads.

No one disputes Aristotle's importance in intellectual history; his influence in logic, metaphysics, ethics, politics, and the study of nature extended across centuries. What Rand exaggerates is the exclusive claim she grants him to represent reason, and his causal role in what followed.

She calls him not only the first great Producer but "the first rational philosopher" in history.[1, p. 6] His role in making logic a systematic and independent field was enormous; the theory of the syllogism was the first comprehensive logical system and shaped the study of valid inference for centuries.[3] But rational philosophy did not begin with him. Presocratic thinkers explained nature through principles internal to nature rather than through mythological genealogies, and Parmenides and Zeno developed detailed arguments about being, change, and motion.[4] Aristotle was the first great system-builder to turn logic into a formal and comprehensive discipline; philosophers reasoned well before him.

Plato constructs arguments, but because he does not arrive at Rand's metaphysical and epistemological conclusions he is excluded from the founding lineage of reason. Being rational comes to mean reaching the results Rand considers correct. His theory of Forms, his division of the sensible and intelligible worlds, and his conception of the soul are all open to criticism from a realist position; Plotinus was a major figure in the Platonic tradition, and Augustine was deeply influenced by Neoplatonism. Equating Plato with mysticism and Aristotle with reason nevertheless produces a slogan. Plato gives a central role to reasoning, the search for definitions, and dialectic; his restrictions on sensible knowledge do not make him an enemy of reason.

Medieval philosophy is not well described as an age of Platonic domination. Current accounts present a multilayered field in which Aristotelian ideas were often transmitted within Platonic contexts.[5] For a long period the Latin West lacked direct access to most of Plato's works; the Platonic inheritance arrived through Augustine, Boethius, Neoplatonic commentaries, and limited translations, while parts of Aristotle's logical corpus had not disappeared. Aquinas was central to the systematic integration of Aristotelian thought into the Latin Christian world, but the process also involved translation movements from Greek and Arabic, the commentaries of Avicenna and Averroes, and thinkers such as Boethius and Albertus Magnus.[5] Aquinas did not use Aristotle to abolish theology, either. He used it to build a new synthesis with Christian doctrine.

Theology suppresses the freedom of reason in Rand's account, yet the hero of reason's return is a systematic Christian theologian. The tension disappears because Aquinas's theology is pushed into the background and his Aristotelianism emphasized. The historical Aquinas is replaced by the Aquinas Rand requires: "the man who brought reason back."

From the thirteenth century onward, Aristotelian thought was central to scholastic philosophy. Aquinas and Albertus Magnus were not stepping outside the Middle Ages by engaging with Aristotle; they were constructing some of the period's most developed philosophical forms. Aristotelianism was one of the constitutive elements of high scholasticism, embedded in the period rather than arriving from outside to end it.

The Renaissance does not divide any more cleanly. Aristotelianism remained powerful in universities and natural philosophy, alongside the revival of Platonism, humanist philology, new translations, the rediscovery of Stoic and Epicurean texts, and transformations in art and education.[6] Platonism and Aristotelianism interpreted and transformed one another as much as they confronted one another.

What organizes Rand's history is a standard that is her own — the lineage a thinker belongs to, more than what he actually argued.

### IV. Kant and the Existentialists

Kant is the great destroyer of modern thought; Zen Buddhism and existentialism are mystical, anti-intellectual doctrines that cannot count as philosophy at all.[1, p. 3] She reduces both to forms their own defenders would not recognize.

Rand argues that Kant severed the mind from reality, that what we perceive are illusions created by mental categories, and that perceiving an object therefore means our perception is false. His system is "cumbersome," "complex," "phony."[1, p. 4]

Kant combines transcendental idealism with empirical realism. We know objects under the conditions of human sensibility and understanding; the world of experience is not thereby an illusion.[7] An appearance is an object as given within the shared conditions of experience; an illusion is an error of judgment or reason. Knowledge shaped by those conditions is still knowledge. Sensibility gives the object, on Kant's account, and the understanding thinks it through concepts; knowledge arises from the unity of the two, not from the mind inventing a world out of its own categories. The Refutation of Idealism argues, further, that outer experience is necessary for self-consciousness.[7] Whether that argument succeeds is a real question; that it exists is enough to show what Kant was attempting.

Serious objections were available to her. The necessity of the categories is disputable; the thing-in-itself is hard to speak of coherently if it is unknowable. Rand instead presents the attempt to establish the limits of knowledge as an abolition of knowledge, and treats Kant as the historical agent of modern intellectual collapse. His influence on later philosophy was profound, but the conclusions reached by his followers are not his own teaching. The noumenon, particularly in its negative sense, marks the limits of sensible cognition rather than naming a mystical domain about which we possess positive knowledge. Later thinkers gave that domain metaphysical interpretations of their own; Rand reads those interpretations back into Kant as his essential meaning.

Her judgment of existentialism is more exclusionary still. Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre, and Beauvoir do not share a conception of religion, metaphysics, human nature, or morality, and only Sartre and Beauvoir explicitly adopted the label.[8] Secular and religious thinkers, advocates of radical freedom and those who restrict it, appear under one heading. To reject a predetermined rational human essence, or an objective order of value, is not to reject thought—one can hold that humanity has no predetermined essence, or that values emerge through choice and action, and still practice systematic and disciplined philosophy. Saying that existentialism is not even philosophy enforces a boundary rather than explaining anything.

Sartre is the useful case, because he resists the type. He and Rand disagree genuinely: she holds that human beings have a determinate nature and that moral values follow from the objective requirements of life; he holds that there is no predetermined essence and that individuals constitute themselves through their choices. The Witch Doctor cannot cope with facts and retreats into emotion, seeking an Attila for protection. Sartre's subject cannot escape responsibility for choice precisely because there is no essence, no God, no ultimate guarantee—a different problem, not an absence of one. His later work acknowledges more explicitly that freedom is constrained by social and historical conditions.[8]

His early theory of emotion complicates the picture further. In *Sketch for a Theory of the Emotions*, emotion is not a passive bodily response but a way in which consciousness "magically" transforms its relation to the world when practical means of coping appear unavailable. Rand objects to substituting emotion for facts; Sartre describes how emotional consciousness may transform the meaning of a situation in place of effective action.[9] One is a phenomenological analysis of consciousness, the other an attack on the epistemic and moral authority granted to emotion—a narrow intersection, but the kind a blanket condemnation cannot see.

A return to Aristotle is a rebirth; interest in Zen, in the same interview, a return to the Dark Ages. The difference is which past is being returned to.

### V. From Emotions to Violence: A Reasonable Distinction, an Invalid Conclusion

At the end of the interview Rand names two premises the New Intellectual must prove to themselves: that emotions are not tools of cognition, and that no one has the right to initiate the use of physical force against another person. She connects them directly. Because emotions cannot be proved, two people acting solely on emotion have no shared means of communication, and unable to appeal to reality as a common arbiter they will resort to blind physical force.[1, pp. 8–9]

Feeling something intensely does not show that one's interpretation is correct. Fear does not establish danger; a sense of certainty does not establish that the idea one defends is true. "This is how I feel" reports an internal condition; it is not evidence for a disputed claim about the external world.

Emotions are not evidence for factual claims; they perform no independent or guiding cognitive function within thought at all; and people who give weight to them cannot communicate and will turn to violence. Rand treats emotions as the "automatic products" of prior thought and value judgments, which captures something real—people fear or grieve or become angry because of the meaning they attribute to an event, and the same event produces different emotions in different people. But the relation does not run in one direction only. Emotional and bodily responses affect which facts attract attention, which possibilities appear threatening, which conclusions carry weight. Bechara and colleagues, in their study using the Iowa Gambling Task, found that participants developed behavioral preferences and physiological reactions before they could articulate the advantageous strategy.[10] Maia and McClelland, using more detailed questioning, later found that participants possessed considerably more conscious knowledge than had been assumed—a result that limits how far the "unconscious bodily guidance" reading can be pushed.[11] Emotional responses can serve as fallible signals in attention and decision, which reason still has to interpret and correct. Fear does not prove a threat exists. It indicates that the person perceives one.

"I am afraid" and "that person will attack me" are not the same kind of statement: the first reports a subjective condition, the second interprets the external world and requires independent testing. Between treating emotion as evidence and silencing it there is the ordinary alternative:

"I was afraid because I thought you were going to attack me."

Anger communicates that a person believes a boundary has been violated, without proving that it was. Rand is right that treating emotion as evidence is an epistemic error; she does not distinguish this from expressing an emotion, which is simply communication. Two people can also accept the same facts and still weigh risks differently, balance freedom and equality differently, or reach the same conclusion from different moral premises, without either of them having retreated from reason.

Uncontrolled anger, fear, or humiliation contribute to violence; not all violence is an outburst. Psychological literature distinguishes reactive aggression, associated with provocation and impulsivity, from proactive aggression, which is premeditated, instrumental, and directed toward a specific gain.[12] A tyrant, an organized-crime leader, or a bureaucrat constructing a system of repression may be calm and calculating. What has gone wrong there is not the capacity to reason but the goals chosen and the moral status granted to other people. Rand could answer that a genuinely rational person would not choose such goals—at which point the claim that reason does not lead to violence stops being a psychological one and becomes true by definition, which is consistent within Objectivism and tells us nothing about the actual psychology of violence.

The non-initiation principle, in any case, does not follow from the theory of emotion. That emotions are not evidence for factual judgments is an epistemological claim. That no one should initiate physical force against another is a moral and political one, and reaching it requires individual rights, bodily integrity, autonomy, and equal moral status—none of which the premise about emotion supplies.

### VI. Science, Capitalism, and the Businessman

Rand describes the major achievements of science as products of the Industrial Revolution, capitalism, and a free society. The businessman is the creative intermediary who turns discovery into a product, organizes mass production, and lifts the standard of living; modern intellectuals, in her account, have treated him as a mindless brute, and businessmen have become the greatest victims of contemporary society.[1, pp. 6–7]

A law of nature does not reach a medicine cabinet on its own, and Rand's businessman closes part of that distance. The Royal Society held its first meeting in 1660, *Philosophical Transactions* began publication in 1665, and Newton's *Principia* appeared in 1687[13], institutionalizing experiment and publication a full century before Britain's classical period of industrialization. Industrial capitalism gave existing science a wider stage and steadier funding; it did not invent the science itself.

Nor did the discoveries reach mass production through the businessman alone. Meisenzahl and Mokyr point to the large body of skilled mechanics and artisans on whom Britain's industrialization depended—the people responsible for the small adaptations that made a working principle into a working machine.[14] A theoretical insight becomes an object in the hands of many people at once: the artisan who builds it, the worker who finds what fails, the buyer who wants it to do something slightly different. Rand might call all of them Producers. In the interview, one of them receives the credit.

She places the professional intellectual in the secondary position of a transmission belt carrying the philosopher's ideas into society, and then praises the businessman for doing exactly the same thing with scientific discoveries. Teachers and critics interpret and transform what they transmit no less than manufacturers do. Carrying a discovery into production counts, in her hierarchy, as creation; carrying an idea into society counts as service—a distinction that has less to do with the two activities than with which one she has already decided to admire.

Her later qualification cuts the other way. Businessmen are Producers, she says, "or they should be."[1, p. 6] A few sentences on, they have become, without qualification, both the creators of modern prosperity and the greatest victims of contemporary society. Success confirms the type; failure is quietly excused from it. The claim to victimhood follows the same rule—the automatic verdict of a hierarchy in which the greatest creator of value is owed the greatest recognition.

Rand grants that a scientist's profession makes them rational, then adds that most of them turn mystical the moment they leave the laboratory.[1, p. 6] A Kantian, a religious, or a socialist scientist all belong to this class as she describes it, at which point the claim is no longer really about scientists: it becomes a way of keeping her theory of reason intact regardless of what practising scientists think of it.

Aristotle establishes reason, science turns it into discovery, the businessman turns discovery into a product, capitalism raises humanity's standard of living—a chain that achieves its clarity by making most historical actors invisible.

### VII. The Founding and the Ideal

When Rand's political history reaches America, the earlier oppositions converge in a national symbol. The Founding Fathers united thought and action and understood that people should deal with one another through trade and persuasion rather than force. From this she draws two conclusions: America was the first and only free society in history, and the economic corollary of its political system was total, unregulated laissez-faire capitalism.[1, p. 7]

The founding was a transformative project with respect to individual rights, representation, written constitutionalism, and limits on governmental power. A major advance toward freedom relative to earlier examples is a comparative judgment, a matter of degree. First and only is a classification, and Rand does not say what would justify moving from one to the other.

The Constitution of 1787 did not use the word slavery, but it wrote the institution into the constitutional settlement. Three-fifths of "all other Persons" were counted for representation. Congress was barred from prohibiting the importation of enslaved people before 1808. Persons "held to Service or Labour" who escaped from one state were not to be discharged from that condition.[15] Constitutional abolition throughout the country required the Thirteenth Amendment, ratified after the Civil War in 1865.[16] By Rand's own standard, slavery is among the most extreme violations of the principle that no one should live under physical force or be denied the right to act on personal judgment.

Slavery, one could argue on her behalf, contradicted the fundamental principles of the American system, and the argument carries real force if individual rights are meant to be universal. But a principle that morally requires abolition is different from a society that has already achieved it. Freedom measured only by the position of the citizens who held it leaves out the boundary that decided who counted as a citizen at all. What survives instead is a narrower reading—a political ideal, newly defining government as the protection of individual rights, rather than a claim about the historical population itself. On this reading "America was free" is a normative interpretation, not a historical description, and it rests on Rand's own theory of rights and capitalism to say what the founding principles correctly understood would require.

The Constitution grants Congress power to collect taxes and duties, borrow money, regulate foreign and interstate commerce, coin money, establish post roads, and grant authors and inventors exclusive rights for limited periods.[15] Federal instruments of taxation, debt, currency, commerce, and intellectual property were present from the beginning—well short of unlimited economic control, and further still from anything like the separation of state and economics.

Nor was the founding generation agreed on laissez-faire. Hamilton's 1791 *Report on Manufactures* discussed tariffs, bounties, and public incentives for domestic production.[17] The First Bank of the United States, chartered by federal statute the same year, was one-fifth publicly owned, the remainder held by private investors, and served as the government's fiscal agent.[18] Rand acknowledges that pure capitalism has never been fully practiced, that constitutional loopholes and contradictions allowed collectivist influences to grow.[1, p. 7] An ideal that has never existed in practice is correspondingly difficult to test against history.

Economic successes, in this account, follow from capitalist freedom; economic failures follow from intervention and the mixed economy. Political freedom follows from American principles; slavery and coercion are departures from them. The classification runs only one way. A regime's self-description does not prove that it applies the theory it names, and a society may certainly act against its own principles—but only if the criteria distinguishing a system's natural products from external interference are fixed in advance. Sorted after the result is already known, the theory cannot be tested against any history at all.

### VIII. Happiness as a Verdict

She reads the "right to the pursuit of happiness" as the right to determine one's own purposes, choose one's values, and work to achieve them, and defines happiness as the state of consciousness arising from the achievement of one's values—not momentary pleasure, but self-esteem, pride in achievement, the enjoyment of life. She then adds a limitation: this is possible only for a rational person living according to a rational moral code.[1, p. 8]

The right does not oblige the state or other people to supply happiness; it means the person must not be forcibly prevented from choosing goals and directing life by his own judgment. A stretch of short-term pleasures may feel directionless; a difficult period spent on a demanding purpose may feel meaningful despite the fatigue.

The pluralism does not survive the move from politics to morality. Not all chosen values, on Rand's account, are real values—only those compatible with reason and the objective requirements of human life can produce genuine happiness, and a life built on mystical belief, self-sacrifice, or mindless pleasure cannot, even if freely chosen. She holds both propositions at once: the right to choose one's own values, and a content of real values already settled by rational morality.

Someone may pursue a goal because it is meaningful, or to please a family, or because no other kind of life seems imaginable, and reach it, and find that the life realized was never his own. Rand's likely response is that such a goal was not a real or rational value. But Rand does not, strictly speaking, determine whether a value is rational by asking whether its achievement produced happiness. Rational values are supposed to be identified independently, by the requirements of human life. She then makes the stronger claim: if a person achieves what is good by that rational standard, it will necessarily make them happy.

That makes the theory vulnerable to a different test. If a value can be independently identified as rational, but its achievement does not produce happiness, the claimed necessary connection between rational value and happiness has failed.

Sheldon and Elliot's self-concordance model, by contrast, finds that goals aligned with a person's own interests and core values are associated with more sustained effort and greater well-being[19]—a result that converges partly with Rand's emphasis on purposeful living without tying well-being to the conclusions of one moral system.

The interview says the least about everything that is not achievement: purpose, pride, and self-esteem stand at the centre, while relationships, dependence, vulnerability, and circumstances beyond a person's control remain in the background. This assumes something the essay is not entitled to treat as neutral: that love, friendship, health, security, care given and received, and loss belong in any adequate account of living well. It is itself a position, not a correction, and it implies that a person may be successful, independent, and self-confident while profoundly lonely, just as another may have no conspicuous achievement and still find life good. Rand's conception of productivity is broad enough that she need not dismiss the second life as worthless; the interview simply has no room for it. Self-esteem, in particular, carries a weight it cannot bear alone. Someone who acquires power, crushes rivals, or exploits others may feel proud, and Rand would not call this rational self-esteem—the adjective settling the question in advance, since rational morality is what determines which values are correct.

McConnell asks, at one point, whether it is important not to feel guilty about one's own happiness. Rather than take up the concept, Rand replies that what matters, in her terms, is being moral.[1, p. 8] Feeling guilty and being guilty are different things, and the question McConnell asked was the harder of the two. Nothing in her answer requires virtue to be a burden — being moral, for Rand, does not mean erasing oneself or being ashamed of one's own happiness. What it cannot do is guarantee that happiness: someone honest and productive may grieve for years, and someone who behaves badly may have power, wealth, and satisfaction that her system can only call counterfeit.

### Proving It to Oneself

Rand says that a genuine intellectual will not accept a philosophy on faith or on the arbitrary authority of another person. Anyone who wants to become an intellectual, she then says, must first prove to themselves and accept two principles that constitute the minimum requirements of civilization: emotions are not tools of cognition, and no one has the right to initiate physical force. These are not axioms. "A man first has to prove them to himself," and only then will his mind be free to consider other philosophical questions.[1, pp. 8–9]

She does not ask for belief on her authority. She asks for independent reason. But the determination lies a level below the asking: can someone examine these premises and conclude that they are false, incomplete, or only partly correct? The structure of the demand leaves little room for it. Proving the premises to oneself is not the same task as asking whether they are true.

---

### Conclusion — Is Reason a Method or a Loyalty Test?

The interview holds together because one word is doing two jobs. As a method, reason is what people who disagree can still share. As Rand uses it, reason is also the name of a particular system—a theory of emotion, rational egoism, productivity, individual rights, laissez-faire capitalism—so that disagreeing with any part of it counts as a departure from reason itself, rather than as a different argument.

The same doubling decides what counts as evidence: slavery becomes a departure from American principle, tariffs and the national bank departures from pure capitalism, a scientist's disagreement a mysticism carried outside the laboratory.

At the end of the interview she declares the contemporary intellectuals dead and reformulates the old phrase:

"The intellectuals are dead—long live the intellectuals!"[1, p. 9]

Whether reason is an open method of inquiry whose destination is not known in advance, or a test that counts as reason only when it arrives at Ayn Rand's conclusions, is the question the interview leaves for whoever answers that call.
